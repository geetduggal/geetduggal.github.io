Folder over Agent, File over App

2026-08-02 — read on Medium

Your AI assistant doesn't need your structure. You still do. (And my foray into a custom hands-free voice agent.)

Image Source: "The Semantic Web", Scientific American circa May, 2001

I still have a vivid memory as a young high school graduate (now a quarter century ago) picking up a copy of The Scientific American magazine. It featured a fascinating article called "The Semantic Web" calling me to immerse myself in what was certainly the future. "You had me at 'Scientific' and 'Web'"! I was more excited than the OG PC World articles on Windows 95.

Image Source: Paul Gray Personal Computing Museum

The article opens with a story:

The entertainment system was belting out the Beatles' "We Can Work It Out" when the phone rang. When Pete answered, his phone turned the sound down by sending a message to all the other local devices that had a volume control. His sister, Lucy, was on the line from the doctor's office: "Mom needs to see a specialist and then has to have a series of physical therapy sessions. Bi-weekly or something. I'm going to have my agent set up the appointments." Pete im- mediately agreed to share the chauffeuring. At the doctor's office, Lucy instruct- ed her Semantic Web agent through her handheld Web browser. The agent promptly retrieved information about Mom's prescribed treatment from the doctor's agent, looked up several lists of providers, and checked for the ones in-plan for Mom's insurance within a 20-mile radius of her home and with a rating of excellent or very good on trusted rating services. It then began trying to find a match between available appointment times (supplied by the agents of individual providers through their Web sites) and Pete's and Lucy's busy schedules.

Here, "Lucy instructed her handheld agent" to get all sorts of cool shit done. The article mentioned that the Semantic Web would "bring structure to meaningful content" beyond key words. For example:

Dr. Hartman works at this clinic on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays and that the script takes a date range in yyyy-mm-dd format [gotta love that nod to ISO-8601] and returns appointment times. And it will "know" all this without needing artificial intelligence on the scale of 2001's Hal or Star Wars's C-3PO. Instead these semantics were en- coded into the Web page when the clinic's office manager (who never took Comp Sci 101) massaged it into shape using off-the-shelf software for writing Semantic Web pages along with resources listed on the Physical Therapy Association's site.

And that "for the Semantic Web to function, computers must have access to structured collections of information and sets of inference rules to conduct automated reasoning."

The idea was to make the internet legible to machines by having humans tag everything first: formal ontologies, typed relationships, URIs for every concept, a whole layer cake of RDF triples and logic rules sitting under the ordinary web. In the end, it asked millions of people to do labeling work nobody really wanted to do.

Agents finally arrived two decades later from another direction: large language models learned to read the web exactly as humans had already left it: messy, untagged, plain prose. They skipped the tagging step entirely The machine adapted to the mess instead of forcing a little structure to the mess. The dream wasn't wrong that agents were coming. It was wrong about what they'd need from us to get here.

See this particularly nice piece by Sean Falconer for more on this topic:

From Ontologies to Agents: The Semantic Web's Quiet Rebirth — Semantic Web meets AI: a new era of agents blends logic, learning, and real-time data to build a web that finally… (seanfalconer.medium.com)

While all this agent talk went in, then out, and then back in the conversation, a revolution on personal knowledge management was taking place. People were starting to organize most of their data digitally in files and folders in the cloud. All tagged and searchable. Bits became cheap and for the first time you could literally hoard everything you like without feeling too guilty about it taking up too much physical space.

The prescription for managing personal and shared data in the cloud came from the opposite end: not structured like the semantic web. You no longer need to structure your documents anymore. Just search, tag, link, and enjoy the ride. Everything is Miscellaneous.

But digging deeper, descriptive research showed that, left alone with no instruction, people naturally organize their own folders into a shallow hierarchies. Ofer Bergman's research on personal file navigation found a mean depth of 2.86 folders and a natural branching of roughly ten subfolders wide, across hundreds of participants and thousands of retrieval steps, none of them told to organize that way.

Put it simply, people tend to not nest folders very deep and not have too many subfolders. An analogy may be like rooms in a house. The numbers of folders and nesting are small enough for the mind to hold in working memory and play with spatially. Some houses have lots and lots of rooms, but they are rare, and probably equally rare are houses with rooms within rooms within rooms.

Bergman's model also found a real cost behind 'too many things in a folder' or 'too much nesting'. For example, retrieval time climbs once folders hold much more than about twenty items, and it turns out users already comply with that ceiling without being told, averaging 22 items per folder.

From a completely different direction (and seemingly independently), Johnny Decimal arrived at nearly the same structure by design rather than observation. The system caps itself at ten areas, ten categories per area, three levels total, full stop. Its creator is explicit that the friction is intentional: limiting yourself to ten forces a moment of consideration before a new category gets created at all.

Line the two up and the match is striking. Bergman's organic mean depth, 2.86, sits alongside Johnny Decimal's designed ceiling of three. Bergman's organic branching, 10.64 subfolders, cozies-up with Johnny Decimal's designed cap of ten.

One data set describes what people already do. One system prescribes what people should do. And they agree almost to the decimal (I know, I just couldn't help myself).

That gap between them is not a productivity tip. It is evidence that the shallow, broad shape is naturally occurring and also a potentially useful prescription. It is what the mind already reaches for when left alone, the same way a house naturally sorts into a handful of simple rooms rather than a population of Russian-doll-style nested rooms.

Enter Folder over Agent. Folder over Agent is a playful play on File over App by Steph Ango (currently CEO of Obsidian): "the files you create are more important than the tools you use to create them". In the same sense, I argue that the durable folder is more important than the AI agents that operate within them.

This is not a new instinct, even if it has rarely been written as beautifully and succinctly as Ango's. 'Local-first software' (the principles Martin Kleppmann and Ink & Switch laid out) makes the same bet in 'sync architecture': the server should be in a supporting role, not the source of truth, so your data survives the company that hosted it. The 'Tools for Thought' lineage, from Niklas Luhmann's slip-box through Andy Matuschak's public notes, assumes the same kinda spiritual stance in personal knowledge management. IndieWeb's 'POSSE principle' (publish on your own site first and syndicate elsewhere) adds a wholesomeness to the topic when applied to your home on the web. Friends are more important than federation. Durable, human-owned, legible structure outlasts whatever transient tool or agent or publishing medium is currently operating on it.

Back to folders. Setting aside the fact that from a filesystem perspective folders are as durable as files, "folder over agent" sounds like a taxonomic argument: organize your files well and the agents will work better and you can retrieve information faster. I'd argue that that's not only a surface-level view, but the former argument on the agent being more useful is likely not even correct.

The actual claim of Folder over Agent is that the folder is a useful representative for accumulated personal context: the compartments a mind uses to feel located, and the parts of life that persist no matter which AI happens to be occupying the room this month. The hierarchy is just the visible, legible surface of something more personal underneath. It does not explain why choosing it yourself, again and again, over letting something else decide it for you, would still matter once an agent can navigate any structure equally well.

That is what this piece is about. 🍿

To put it more bluntly, these days a perfectly fluent agent does not need the folder to be organized. Hell, it can even suggest really useful organizations for you! It can find anything regardless of where it sits, structured or flat, labeled or piled.

That's great news, but it removes you from being involved in the organization of own files in the same way it would you deciding where stuff is kept in your home. If the agent needed your structure to function, you would be forced to keep tending it. Since it does not need your structure at all, the only reason left to cultivate your home is that you actually want to.

Consider the actual moments where organizing a folder does something. Archiving a finished project into something named "Old" is a small ritual of closure, not a retrieval aid, since the agent could already find those files wherever they sat. Naming a folder "Mom" instead of "Family_Contacts_2026" only matters because the name is true to how you think, not because it changes what gets found. Splitting "Health" into "Training" and "Recovery" is a moment of noticing something true about your activity that is meaningful to you personally. None of these decisions have a more mundane functional payoff. They exist because you wanted to know, for yourself, what something was.

To be a little more concrete, consider an example folder called "Misc" that sits untouched for two years, because nothing ever forced it open. One evening, for no operational reason, it gets opened anyway. Half of it turns out to be dead weight, deleted without a second thought. The other half is a real thread, a hard year never quite named while it was being lived. Those files get pulled out, and the folder gets a name you feel is right. The process takes maybe 11 minutes, start to finish. The agent found those files just as fast when they were buried in "Misc." What those minutes produced was not better retrieval. It was a judgment: this mattered to me, that didn't, and this is what I'm going to call it.

This is what it means to curate your data with care. A machine can sort files by date or type in half a second. I'd argue that it should be your choice to decide whether a stretch of your life deserves its own name and you get value from that that compounds over time and works well with your memory.

Stepping back and on a more personal level, the philosophy is Folder over Agent not Folder no Agent. I am an enthusiastic user of AI tools and agents and am particularly fond of hands-free voice agents. They let me brainstorm ideas and do research while being otherwise physically active. It is a great time to be alive and I've evolved and learned more than I ever have before in the most positive sense just due to the existence of these tools.

It's no surprise then that I was a very early adopter of ChatGPT's voice mode (back when it was surprisingly useful and irritating in equal measures) and extensively use Claude's voice mode on a daily basis. While I find them excellent, I also found them lacking for a few particular use cases:

  • Inability to access local files on my phone much like Claude Code / Codex will on a desktop
  • Inability to properly do deeper research and search the web in voice mode (I would often be prompted to go to my desktop or out of voice mode for obvious reasons, but it's not something I wanted to do)
  • Ability to swap between models and local voices
  • Easily work within the context of my folder structure

I therefore decided it was time to modify my homegrown local-first plain text app Order to incorporate a hands-free voice agent that can operate directly on my files and folders that are local to my phone much like Claude Code or Codex would work on my desktop. It has its own system prompt that I can customize to suit my needs. Its core is written in Rust that runs locally on my phone and desktop and is therefore extremely efficient.

The result is rather than working around a large foundation model company's higher level app decisions that are meant to be generally useful, I have a tool that allows me to better invite these agents into my home to help me with something specific as opposed to leaving my home to meet an agent and then import its work into my home. In my view, an agent invited into my home should work like a contractor called in for a job, not an unaccommodating landlord who takes over my house and sense of agency. The agent shows up, does the work asked of it, and leaves. The place still feels like mine and curated by my decisions.

It is in this sense that Folder over Agent is a bet that the small, perhaps functionally unnecessary, human habit of classifying your own life on your own terms is worth keeping, and that the right agent is the one that helps you keep doing it, rather than the one that starts doing it for you. And it is with that sentiment that I hope I have inspired you to be even a little more excited about tending to your own folders. 'Till next time! 🗻
